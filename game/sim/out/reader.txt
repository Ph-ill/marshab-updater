FLIGHT DAY 01
  Jai calls home while the delay is still under a second. Someone tells a bad joke and Jai laughs on the beat, in real time, with everyone at the table. Afterwards Jai writes down who was there and what they ate, and folds the list into a flight-suit pocket.
  Phill and Jacques share the night watch over the fleet link. From low orbit Earth fills the window, its dark side strung with cities. Phill holds up a thumb, and the planet spills out around it on every side. They talk about home as if it were down the road. For a few more days, it is.

FLIGHT DAY 02
  Michelle plays the same four bars on a travel guitar at the end of every watch, into the fleet channel, never the rest of the song. By the last top-off tanker six ships are guessing at the fifth bar, six different ways.
  The ships hold formation a few kilometres apart, trimmed with RCS. One seat a ship, so no one failure takes two people, and the next ship reads back every critical command. Every few days they close up: a tethered line, oxygen first. Dorian has the first crossing and already calls it "educational".
  Michelle and Diego watch the last top-off tankers come and go from their own windows, a few kilometres apart, and narrate each docking on the fleet channel like a sports commentary. By the third one they have a catchphrase, and by the fourth it has gone fleet-wide.

FLIGHT DAY 03
  The galley screen on Kim's ship takes a 2.3-gigabyte update overnight and restarts at breakfast: the logo, a progress bar, the three-note chime, all over before Kim has counted to eleven. Then it logs out again. By departure the whole fleet is humming the chime.
  Phill and Jacques share the exercise slot over video, two hours a day so their bones still work at Mars. Whoever hangs up first owes the other a dessert. The sessions keep getting longer.
  Jai answered mail from home: weather, a dog, a fence that needs mending.

FLIGHT DAY 04
  Dorian plants the garden: six seed pods in a tray the size of a laptop, under a lamp the colour of a plum. Dorian labels each pod in tiny capitals and says good night to them on an open channel. Several people say good night back.
  Kim stands the night watch for the whole fleet so the other six can sleep. Unasked. The watch report is two lines long and perfectly neat.

FLIGHT DAY 05
  Jai reads the manifest like a shopping list: water on Cargo 3, rover on Cargo 4. Write-offs: a drill rig left in Earth orbit; a grey relay, "thruster stuck open"; the last window's hopper, "tipped, beacon intermittent"; Cargo 2, drill spares, "lost on landing, scar imaged"; socks, "reason unknown".
  Phill, Jacques and Michelle cleaned the galley filter and found most of last week's bread.

FLIGHT DAY 06
  The last tanker undocks at dawn. Seven ships light their engines for the departure burn, and Diego feels weight come back for the first time since launch. From here the flight log keeps one page for every five days, and by the next page Diego can hide Earth behind a thumb.
  A replacement pump won't sit in its mount on Jai's ship. Every part is in tolerance. Stacked together, they're 1.4 millimeters out. Jai shims it with a washer from the spares kit and writes the stack-up on the bulkhead for the next person.
  Diego walks Kim through taking the landing by hand over the link: hold, pick a new spot, commit. Step by step, until Kim can call it without the checklist. Diego logs it as training. It is.
  Jacques counted the spare fuses twice and got the same number both times.

FLIGHT DAY 07
  Once a week the fleet eats together on video: seven tablets on seven galley tables, one meal at one time. Michelle is in charge of the toast. It's short, it isn't a joke, and it lands.
  A flare has gone off on the Sun, and its protons will reach the fleet in about forty minutes. Dorian packs into the storm shelter between the water tanks with a deck of cards, and every crew in the fleet does the same, cards over video, until the dosimeter settles.
  For whoever at home looks after Jacques, and only them: An external camera on Jacques's ship has iced over, and its heater can't shift it. Clearing it now means the next ship closing up for an unscheduled window, someone suited on the other end. No one would blame Jacques for waiting.
  Diego and Dorian keep a channel open as Earth and the Moon slide into the same frame, each at their own window on their own ship. For a long time the link carries only the hum of two sets of fans. At the shift change Dorian sends one photo: the Moon, half lit, over a sliver of Earth.
  Just before midnight, 214 alarms hit Phill's console at once. One of them is real.

FLIGHT DAY 08
  The night's record: Phill found it buried on page nine, a coolant pump drawing too much current, minutes from tripping. Phill swapped to the backup before the primary died. By morning the alarm list was down from 214 to a page. The backup is the only pump now.
  The data drop brings Jacques one file flagged personal, from the one person at home who never sends files.
  For whoever at home looks after Jacques, and only them: The airlock is the one place aboard with no camera feed to the ground, so reading it now means reading it there. Saving it means carrying it unread until a quiet moment that may never come.
  For whoever at home looks after Michelle, and only them: Michelle finds a hairline crack in a bracket holding a stowage rack, at a corner the model said would carry no stress. A proper fix means opening a ticket with Earth. A zip-tie fix takes five minutes.
  Dorian waters the garden tray with a syringe, two millilitres per pod, and checks each one under the lamp. Nothing yet. Dorian writes it down: "Nothing yet. Good, even nothing." Then tells the pods so.
  Kim records a message for a school group back home that's been following the fleet. The kids send back forty questions, mostly about the toilet. Kim answers every one.

FLIGHT DAY 09
  The weekly radiation summary puts Diego's dosimeter at the top of the fleet's list, a little higher every week. The bunk on Diego's ship sits on the bare hull, the one wall with no tanks or stowage behind it.
  For whoever at home looks after Diego, and only them: Moving the bunk means telling the flight surgeon, who will want a meeting, and a meeting with the ground gets slower every week.
  For whoever at home looks after Jai, and only them: The attitude control on Jai's ship has a slow wobble only Jai seems to notice. A switch to the alternate gain set in the onboard library would probably settle it. Procedure says the guidance team signs off any switch, and the console on shift wants the responsible engineer's signature.
  Kim and Jai compare storm shelters on video and steal each other's best ideas: one more sleeping spot, a hook for the cards, and a sign, now on every ship, that reads "the Sun is not your friend".
  Phill, Jacques and Michelle re-torqued a hatch hinge to spec and wrote down the number.

FLIGHT DAY 10
  Jacques went with your call: Wait for the planned EVA. Jacques waits. The camera stays blind for a few days, which the risk assessment calls acceptable. Jacques has read all of it, appendix included.
  With the cabin dark and the Sun behind the hull, Dorian sees the zodiacal light with no air in the way: a long faint cone of comet dust, lit from behind. From home it is a smudge at dusk. Out here it is a road. Dorian turns the screen brightness down to keep it.
  For whoever at home looks after Phill, and only them: The airlock on Phill's ship won't cycle. One door sensor says "closed". The other says "ajar". The interlock won't move until they agree. The door is obviously closed, and Phill has the override code.
  Jai and Kim spend a whole shift arguing on the fleet channel about what plays during the next burn. Jai wants silence, so every noise means something. Kim wants anything with a chorus. They settle on one song, chosen by the ground team, sight unseen. It's worse than either.

FLIGHT DAY 11
  Jacques went with your call: Read it now. Jacques reads it in the airlock and comes out ten minutes later without saying what it said. That evening Jacques calls the other ships one by one, about nothing in particular.
  Michelle went with your call: Open a ticket. Earth wants photos, measurements, and a part number that isn't on any drawing. Michelle sends all three. The approved repair arrives a few days later and is, in every way that matters, a zip tie.
  Behind a stowage panel on Phill's ship, three ration packs that never made the manifest. Someone at Starbase wrote "you'll thank me" on the bag in Sharpie, next to a little drawing of a Starship with its fingers crossed.
  For whoever at home looks after Phill, and only them: The three packs from behind the panel are the good rations, the ones the galley ran out of first. They aren't on any inventory.
  Jacques runs the dosimeter check. Every crew member is now past fifty millisieverts, and the line is still climbing at the same slope. Jacques writes it down and underlines it, and leaves the underline to say the rest.
  Michelle wakes from a dream of rain on a car roof and lies still, trying to keep the sound. The fans hum. The ship ticks as it turns into the sun. By breakfast the rain has gone. Michelle writes "rain, car roof" in the log margin, in case it comes back.

FLIGHT DAY 12
  No word from home in time, so Diego chose: Keep it quiet. Diego notes it privately and moves the sleeping bag to the other wall. It isn't nothing. It isn't a meeting either.
  Jai went with your call: Wait for sign-off. Jai files the request and lives with the wobble overnight. The ground approves Jai's change, word for word, the next afternoon.
  Transfer window. The next ship's hatch takes a code that goes stale in a fifth of a second. The procedure still says to read them up via the ground. Michelle does, once: CODE STALE. Ship to ship it opens first time, and Michelle files a correction to page two.
  The galley screen on Jacques's ship has started to sulk: after every restart it sits on its logo longer than it used to, then boots with a sad cloud icon and the word OFFLINE. Jacques files a ticket titled "manners".
  The CO₂ scrubber on Dorian's ship throws its fourth fault since the hatch closed on the pad. Dorian fixes it, then labels it "temperamental" in permanent marker, under a label in Starbase handwriting that already says "be nice to it".
  Diego's wristwatch is still on home time. At fleet midnight Diego glances at it and tells the net, "Breakfast at home." The answer comes back the same, from a different ship each time: "Enjoy."
  Phill and Kim ran the comms self-test and filed a clean sheet.

FLIGHT DAY 13
  Phill went with your call: Find a third opinion. Phill pulls the lock's pressure trace as a tiebreaker. The door is sealed, and the "ajar" sensor is lying. Phill swaps it, the interlock is satisfied, and nothing was overridden.
  Fleet decision open for two pages. Both options are cleared; the flight director wants the fleet's call. Water recovery on Michelle's ship is down to 93 percent from 98. Michelle wants to strip the processor now. Diego wants to run it degraded and fix it when things are calmer. [Strip it now / Run it degraded]
  After a long debate on the fleet channel, Dorian and Phill get all seven ships to agree on which drawer the zip ties live in. It has taken the whole flight so far, and it may be the most important engineering decision made out here.
  Michelle inventories the spares locker by hand and finds a scrubber cartridge the manifest says was never loaded. Somebody at Starbase packed a margin and ticked the bag in Sharpie. Michelle logs it, with a thank-you in the margin.

FLIGHT DAY 14
  Phill went with your call: Keep them quiet. Phill thanks the Sharpie silently. The packs go into a personal locker, for the bad week every mission eventually has.
  Jacques cleans up the receiver's noise floor for the weekly report and finds a faint ripple in it, at about nine hundredths of a hertz. A control loop hunting somewhere, most likely the coolant pump's: it's on the same bus. Jacques notes it in the margin and moves on to the propellant tables.
  Something hits Jai's ship hard enough to tick through the whole hull. Every pressure reading is green. Jai doesn't trust green.

FLIGHT DAY 15
  Decision closed: Run it degraded. Votes cast: 4. The fleet goes on water rationing. The complaints stay off the channel. Michelle writes down the recovery rate every hour; it slips a tenth at a time.
  Jai's morning EVA, Kim suited at the next ship's hatch, found a fingernail crater in a heat-shield tile, short of the bondline. The entry team clears it on one condition: Jai's ship flies entry a few tenths of a degree steeper, for a sharper peak and a shorter soak.
  Kim's arrival-test dish scans the old common channel toward Mars; no one has listened there since the relay was written off. A week of integration shows a faint carrier. Folded at 10.96 seconds, 2.4 percent duty, it stands up like a fence post. "An alias, probably," Kim says.
  Fleet decision open for two pages. Both options are cleared; the flight director wants the fleet's call. A gamma-ray burst goes off far past the galaxy, and every ship's X-ray monitor catches it. Timed against detectors near Earth, the raw buffers could pin the source, but pulling them home bumps a day of mail. Phill wants the data sent. Jacques wants the letters. [Send the data / Letters first]

FLIGHT DAY 16
  A voice note from the Starbase loading crew reaches Michelle, recorded on a forklift by the sound of it. The one with the Sharpie: "Eat the good rations first. The bad ones keep. And say hello to the hardware. Ours won't start till you do." Michelle checks the locker. True.
  The CO₂ scrubber on Dorian's ship fails outright. With one person in a cabin that size, the CO₂ only climbs about a twentieth of a percent a day: not an emergency yet, which is the best time to fix one. Dorian has the backup running in nine minutes, and it is already complaining.
  Anyone can answer this one. One tap is enough. Dorian's ship is breathing on its backup scrubber. It needs a spare cartridge before the backup gives out. [Send a cartridge / Jai brings it over]

FLIGHT DAY 17
  Decision closed: Send the data. Votes cast: 4. The burst's source shrinks from a smear of sky to a thin arc, and the arc runs through one faint galaxy. Phill saves the confirmation in the photo folder.
  Help is on the way: Send a cartridge. A spare cartridge rides the line across in an unscheduled transfer window. Dorian's ship is breathing easy by evening.
  Phill looks up the grey relay. Its thruster stuck open just before the cargo landings; three power-only passes afterwards, on a spare antenna, caught it spinning every 10.96 seconds, beam on Earth 2.4 percent of a turn. Nothing else used the channel. Phill sketches a lighthouse in blue.
  Jacques reads the fleet's dose charts like the news, notices Diego's numbers, and calls. Jacques's dosimeter has the newer calibration, and the two of them redo Diego's week with it. That night Diego moves the bunk in beside the water tanks, and Jacques stays on the line until it's done.
  The hatch on Kim's ship sticks whenever the sunward side warms up. Kim works it out on the back of a checklist: the frame grows a millimeter in the sun and the seal doesn't. One new shim, and the door swings the way it did at Starbase.
  Diego and Dorian try to bake bread in the galley oven while the visit lasts. Without gravity it rises in every direction at once and comes out shaped like a lung. They eat all of it, warm, with the lights down, and agree not to describe it to anyone at home.

FLIGHT DAY 18
  The degraded water processor on Michelle's ship quits at 04:00. Kim rides the transfer line over in an unscheduled window with the spare valve, and the two of them have water flowing by breakfast. It tops out at ninety-five percent, for good now.
  The delay has turned calls into letters. Jai records one for home and talks straight through the gaps, then deletes it and does it again with pauses, which is worse, and then a third time at speed, list and all, which is the one that goes. The list from the first call is still in the pocket.
  Four of the six pods have gone soft and grey: drowned. The schedule came from a book written for windowsills, where water drains away. Up here it just sits. Dorian lifts them out with tweezers. "Wrong book," Dorian tells the tray. "Let's go again." Half a dose, now, for the other two.
  Fleet decision open for two pages. Both options are cleared; the flight director wants the fleet's call. Mission Control proposes moving the fleet's clocks two hours forward, to line up with the ground team's shifts. Kim is for it. Jai has seen this study before. [Change the clocks / Keep fleet time]
  It's Phill's birthday by the ground calendar, and Jacques is still aboard from the last crossing. Jacques produces a candle that can't be lit, a cake that is a ration bar with a slice of dried mango on it, and a card signed by the whole fleet. Phill keeps the candle.
  01:50. The cabin pressure on Michelle's ship is falling, a tenth of a kilopascal an hour. Michelle is awake to see it first, a minute before the fleet's night watch.

FLIGHT DAY 19
  Michelle spent four hours panel by panel with a leak detector, suit staged at the hatch. The culprit was a cable feedthrough whose seal shrank in the shaded-side cold. The trend goes in the logbook like a fish someone caught; under it, the reserve line, which won't recover before Mars.
  Diego spots a glint keeping pace off the starboard side, catching the sun every few seconds. Diego notes its bearing hourly. By the fourth hour it's plainly tumbling, each flash a face of ice from the last waste-water dump, a little smaller each time. The notes are neat enough to frame.
  Movie night with Phill, on a tablet stuck to the ceiling: Jacques picked a film set on a submarine. Halfway through, the hull ticks as the ship turns out of the sun, right on cue. Jacques pauses it, and the two of them listen until it stops. Then Jacques puts on something with a sky in it.

FLIGHT DAY 20
  Decision closed: Keep fleet time. Votes cast: 4. The fleet keeps its clocks. The ground team adjusts instead, and sends a photo of the night shift's pizza.
  Kim asks Diego to point a second dish at the carrier. The bursts land on both in the same millisecond and slide against fleet time by about a second and a half a day, the fleet's closing rate on Mars. Hour to hour, the residuals are flat. Kim books the dish again.
  Fleet decision open for two pages. Both options are cleared; the flight director wants the fleet's call. If it's the grey relay, it may still know a standard hello. Kim wants clearance to answer every burst with one, signed with the old program key the fleet carries as a relay fallback: a courtesy, and a test. Diego points out that every reply ties up the comms computer. [Answer every burst / File it, keep going]
  Jai kills every cabin light, shades the Sun-side window and waits twenty minutes for night eyes. The Milky Way comes up with the dark lanes in it, the ones no backyard ever shows. Jai names the ones in the book, then starts on the rest.
  The protein-crystal experiment in Phill's locker, a university lab's two years of work, cooks overnight when its freezer fan seizes. Phill reads the temperature trace, then calls the lab, who ask for photos anyway. There is no second batch, and no second flight for it.

FLIGHT DAY 21
  On the night Diego finally asks about the four bars, Michelle plays them again, all four, and stops. "Planet first." Diego says that's a deal, and starts humming a made-up fifth bar on purpose.
  Michelle teaches Kim a card game from home across a real galley table, not a screen. By the end of the shift Kim is winning, and the rules have been amended three times.
  Jacques, Jai and Dorian cleaned the galley filter and found most of last week's bread.

FLIGHT DAY 22
  Decision closed: Answer every burst. Votes cast: 3. The ground clears it inside a day: hello only, nothing that commands. Kim adds one line to the comms schedule, and the first hello goes out at 14:02, timed to the millisecond. Diego files one line of dissent, signed, and leaves it there.
  Through the long lens Earth and the Moon fit in one frame now, a blue dot and a grey one, close as two buttons on a coat. Jai shoots the pair once a shift for a week and sends the frames home as a flipbook, captioned "You are here. Both of you."
  The galley pump fails for the third time this voyage. Jai lays the parts out on a towel in order, rebuilds it, and the washer that was missing at the factory turns up in the old housing.
  Dorian shuts both eyes in the bunk and sees a white spark, then another. Cosmic rays, passing straight through the eye, fast enough to make light; cameras see them too, as single bright pixels. Dorian counts them like sheep and loses count somewhere in the twenties.
  Dorian counts the medical kit against the manifest: sutures, splints, pain tablets in neat blister rows. Dorian counts it once more and closes the lid gently.

FLIGHT DAY 23
  Michelle spends a slow day re-stowing the whole ship to the arrival plan, locker by locker. When the others get to theirs, Michelle's list is already on the fleet channel, with tips.
  Jai clips a new blank postcard inside the flight-plan binder every page, a different city each time, nothing on the back. The fleet guesses where from. Jai has confirmed one, and won't say which.

FLIGHT DAY 24
  It's been a long week. Phill opens the locker before breakfast, tags one pack for Jacques for the next transfer window, and eats the other on video with Jacques, reading the Sharpie on the bag aloud like the back of a cereal box.
  Jacques plots a week of the old-channel carrier for the anomaly file. It isn't steady: there for part of each day, gone for the rest, and back thirty-seven minutes later each morning. Jacques blames the dish's scan schedule and moves on to the dust forecast.
  During Diego's scheduled EVA a tether snags on a bracket and wrenches Diego's arm, with the next ship closed up and a suited crewmate clipped in at its hatch. Diego frees it, works back along the line hand over hand, and comes inside with a wrist that won't bend.
  Anyone can answer this one. One tap is enough. Diego needs the ultrasound probe from another ship's medical kit, and the next scheduled transfer window is days away. [Send the kit / Jacques brings it over]
  Jai swept the cabin with the leak detector and found nothing, which is the point.
  Mission Control misses a scheduled check-in. Then the next one. Kim sends a third hail and starts the clock: twice the light time, plus a minute for someone to put a coffee down.

FLIGHT DAY 25
  Help is on the way: Send the kit. The fleet holds formation tight for an unscheduled transfer, and the kit rides the line across alone. Diego's wrist is scanned and splinted by lunch, and the anomaly report runs to four pages.
  The reply arrived before breakfast. A ground station had gone down in a storm at home, and the backup had to wait for Earth to turn. For eight hours the fleet was on its own, and the checklist for that is one page long. The message ends: "All good here. How are you?" Kim starts with the weather.
  Stacked over a week of bursts, the receiver pulls out half a frame of housekeeping on the old channel, in a format the receiver has to look up, cut off partway. The fleet's answers go out on schedule, every one. Kim forwards the scrap home, and Mission Control replies six minutes later: "say again."
  The ground can't run the grey relay forward: its orbit data is two years stale. "Check the dish schedule at your end," the ground says. Phill slides the lighthouse sketch into the flight-plan binder.
  The school group writes again: they've built Mars in the classroom out of papier-mâché and want to know if it's the right red. Michelle sends back a photo through the window, unprocessed, and a note: "Close. A bit more butterscotch."

FLIGHT DAY 26
  Jacques has read the letter so often that the fold has worn through. Jacques mends it along the crease and keeps it in the flight-suit pocket over the heart, which the checklist calls the "left chest pocket".
  Under the last tray in the spares locker, Jai finds a roll of tape that isn't on any list, and under it a laminated card in a bag: an old handshake procedure, step by step, headed in the same Sharpie hand: "Old manners, for old hardware." Beside it, the little ship again, fingers crossed.
  A dried leaf in a plastic sleeve rides in Jacques's chest pocket, picked up on the walk to the pad. Every few days Jacques checks it and reports to the net: "Still green on one side." The ground files it as a status.

FLIGHT DAY 27
  A message from home for Jai: a funeral, already over, for the neighbour who always brought the folding chairs, a name from the first call's list. By the time it arrived, everyone in the photos had gone home and changed. Jai watches the recording to the end and asks for the next watch.
  Phill, Jacques, Kim, Michelle and Dorian wiped down the window and watched Mars not move.

FLIGHT DAY 28
  A batch of photos from the Starbase loading crew reaches Dorian: the bay, the cranes, the team in hi-vis, a named forklift charging off a cargo ship's landing leg. The one with the Sharpie signs off: "Seven of you this time. Write when you land." Dorian clips the forklift inside the logbook.
  Long table night. Dorian has the toast and gives it to the empty chair at each of the seven tables, the one where a second crew member would sit on a normal ship. It lands on every screen at once, the one conversation out here with no delay in it. Jai laughs on the beat, and it counts double.
  Fleet decision open for two pages. Both options are cleared; the flight director wants the fleet's call. The backup-site maps, stitched from imagery older than the cargo landings (the only sharp camera at Mars rode on the grey relay), show an unnamed crater small enough for the fleet to name. Phill wants to name it after the first person who ever wrote a Mars mission plan. Kim wants somewhere at home. [Honor the planners / Name it for home]
  23:58. Michelle's ship stops answering on the fleet net in the middle of a sentence. No telemetry, just an empty slot on six displays. Through the next ship's long lens, its cabin windows are lit as usual.

FLIGHT DAY 29
  Michelle was back inside three hours. A frame on the old channel, from the listening dish, had hung the comms computer; the backup hung on the same frame, and the third boot quarantined it. The ground moves the channel to a sandbox on the dish; the hellos go on from a box that can't hang a radio.
  Phill, Jacques and Jai slept nine hours on the flight surgeon's orders.

FLIGHT DAY 30
  A tie, 4 votes in all. The crew broke it themselves: Honor the planners. The name goes in the request. Somewhere, a committee will argue about it for years, which Phill considers part of the honor.
  Dorian pulls an old survey orbiter's low-light night frames of the landing region. On dark ground near the edge of one frame, one pixel is lit. On the next frame it isn't. A cosmic-ray hit on the sensor, almost certainly. Dorian logs it with the other sensor hits.
  Phill, Jacques and Michelle walked the hull with the camera and found it whole.

FLIGHT DAY 31
  Since the fleet kept its own clock, the ground's handover lands in the fleet's first coffee, and the night crew's last hour is the fleet's first. Diego knows them all by name now, and they know Diego's coffee order, which is no use to anyone across this much space.
  The surface team sends the landing-site plan, and Diego reads it out. Water first: Cargo 3's ice drill made it down with one bit, rated for a season. The engineer who built it adds a line at the bottom: "Be gentle with it."

FLIGHT DAY 32
  Dorian goes through every night frame of that patch this month. Every pass, on different rows of the sensor, the lit pixel lands on the same spot of ground. Dorian sends the list round. The ground's first guess: the hopper, tipped on its pad, beacon still coming and going.
  Fleet decision open for two pages. Both options are cleared; the flight director wants the fleet's call. Dorian wants to ask the ground to task an orbiter for a fast sequence over the lit pixel, at the cost of the fleet's next deep-space pass, because the orbiter's data needs the same ground antenna: every letter from home waits. Phill would leave it for the people who'll be down there with time to look. [Ask for the sequence / Leave it for the ground]
  23:40, mid-window, with Mars square to Phill's dish. The old-channel carrier misses a knock, the first in-window miss in weeks. Phill counts past eleven, twelve, thirty. The orbiter's pass over that patch comes down an hour later: in its frame, the lit pixel is dark too.

FLIGHT DAY 33
  Decision closed: Ask for the sequence. Votes cast: 4. The request goes up. Dorian writes the apology to six inboxes personally, and means each one.
  It came back six knocks late and still on the beat. Phill lays the gap on the temperature maps: the night side's coldest hour. Each cold night the gap is a knock or two longer. Phill runs the line forward. It reaches entry week.
  The entry mass budget wants the tank beside Diego's bunk pumped down. Diego argues to keep it and runs the dose numbers to prove it. With this little cruise left, they don't. "Fine," Diego tells the ground. "Pump it." Then, because the flight surgeon once asked: "Thanks for checking."
  Earth is five light-minutes away. Jacques asks the ground a yes-or-no question and makes a whole cup of tea before the yes arrives. Conversations have become letters; letters have become weather.
  Through the shade filter the Sun is a coin held further off each week. The solar arrays say it first, a percent or two less on every page. Jai turns the cabin heater up a notch, notes why, and caps the filter on a star that used to be a sky.
  Kim and Michelle chased a rattle behind a panel and found a pen.

FLIGHT DAY 34
  The ground decodes a whole frame from Mars for Diego: a link request on an old program key, sequence 5,750,112, with a fresh challenge. Diego does the sum: two years, at least. The ground: "Old key. The first batch was re-keyed after landing; only the relay kept it. Nothing on it still flies."
  In the restow for entry, item fourteen of the ground's procedure moves personal papers to the checklist binder, letter included. Jacques finds the left chest pocket empty and turns out the locker, sleeping bag and wipes bin before the restow list explains it. Entry page. "Item located," Jacques logs.
  Methane boil-off on Jai's ship runs high while it flies broadside to feed the solar wings. Jai proposes nose-to-Sun, wings canted, so the ship's own shadow falls down the tanks. The ground runs the power numbers and agrees; boil-off drops by a third, and everyone's plan changes.

FLIGHT DAY 35
  The fast sequence comes down overnight: forty frames of dark ground as the orbiter slides over. The pixel lights, goes out, lights again, and never moves against the rocks. Dorian counts the frames between flashes, gets the same number each time, and calls the fleet.
  Jai copies the list from the first call into the entry notes, name by name, and reaches the end feeling it's short. It is: the person who held the phone up that night never made it onto the list, because they were holding the phone. Jai adds them at the top.
  In one of the orbiters' night frames Kim finds a faint green smear over dark ground: Martian aurora, patchy, pinned to old magnetized rock no hand has ever touched. Kim saves the frame and sends it to the geology team, subject line: "look".

FLIGHT DAY 36
  Jacques borrows the old-channel receiver record, slides it back by the one-way light time from Mars, just under half a minute, and lays it over the forty frames. The carrier peaks on the exact frame the pixel lights. Every time. Jacques puts both traces on the galley screen, side by side.
  Alone, with the channel off, Michelle tries a fifth bar after the four and gets it wrong. Then wrong again, differently. Michelle puts the guitar back in its bag. "Not yet," Michelle tells the guitar. "Entry morning."
  Another flare, another forty minutes. Michelle is in the shelter between the water tanks in six, with the cards already dealt for seven screens. By now everyone knows which deck cheats at what, and the dosimeter has stopped being the most interesting number in the room.
  Phill and Jai reran a pump check that came back two tenths off, then on.

FLIGHT DAY 37
  The ground answers the overlay: nothing near that pixel has power. The hopper is ninety kilometres south, its beacon as patchy as ever. Nearest is a scar a few kilometres off, filed two years ago as impact debris. Two weeks of hails on the old key, unanswered. On the list it goes.
  Rationed water was one squeeze too many: the last pod never came up. Dorian keeps watering the tray, two millilitres, on schedule, and packs the dry seed in a coat pocket for the ground. Seeds are patient. So, it turns out, is Dorian.
  Diego runs the first entry seat checks with the wrist that wouldn't bend, mostly bending now, and reports it to the flight surgeon as "bending", with the angle in degrees. The surgeon clears the seat.

FLIGHT DAY 38
  Phill looks at the lighthouse again. Every knock since the first, timed to the microsecond: they drift once a day, by a hundredth of a second, and the day is thirty-seven minutes too long. Under the lighthouse, in blue, Phill draws a question mark.
  The old-channel carrier now stands out in a narrow filter. Michelle turns each burst into a click on the cabin speaker: a click, a hiss, a long pause, a click, like rain starting on a car roof. The four bars fit between them. Michelle plays them on the galley keyboard, stopping where the fifth should be.

FLIGHT DAY 39
  Kim works out how many times the old-channel carrier has knocked since the fleet first heard it: close to half a million. Every gap has a reason, Mars in the way or a cold night, and every knock lands on the beat. "Half a million. Every gap explained. Out," Kim sends.
  Michelle times the old-channel carrier on the galley clock, like lightning to thunder. Ten, eleven, knock. The fleet's round trip to the planet is thirty-odd seconds now, and shrinking. Michelle writes both on the whiteboard, one above the other.
  The entry checklist has a line for "personal items stowed". Jacques reads the last line of the letter once more, aloud, to an empty cabin, then folds it into the left chest pocket, seals the flap, and ticks the box in ink.
  Phobos is a speck beside Mars on the long-lens camera now, crossing its face in under an hour. Kim watches it cross a whole world in the time it takes to eat a meal, and wants weight, suddenly, more than sleep.
  Phill and Jai recalibrated a cabin sensor reading half a degree warm.

FLIGHT DAY 40
  The fleet's long lenses make the first sharp pass over the backup site in two years: behind a crater rim, a ship stands on its legs, tilted, one solar wing half open, dish jammed against its stop, talking on its low-gain antenna: Cargo 2. With each knock its pad strobe flares, then goes dark while it listens. Phill counts the dark: nine, ten, eleven, flare. Ten point nine six: the lighthouse's number.
  The board's finding reaches Dorian as Cargo 2's own patch note, signed off at Starbase: SURVEYED SITE = PAD. ON PAD: OBEY PAD CREW. KNOCK, LISTEN 1 TICK, KNOCK. TIMEOUT: NONE. The board's margin: boulders, a divert behind the rim, down whole; written off before the re-key; no orbiter but the dead relay ever had its channel. Earth, fifteen minutes. The tick, eleven seconds.
  On the new pass Jacques finds the crater the fleet named for the first person who ever wrote a Mars plan, and Cargo 2 behind its rim, where no old map could show it. Somewhere on Earth, a committee is about to have a great deal more to argue about.
  Phill sent Kim the last pack from the locker on the last transfer window, with a note on the bag in borrowed handwriting: "You'll thank me." Kim opens it tonight.

FLIGHT DAY 41
  04:10:07: Cargo 2 knocks. Kim's receiver hears it five seconds later and answers at once, signed. Entry week is frozen, but this hello has been on the schedule, cleared, since the vote: the ground lets it finish the handshake and nothing more, and Cargo 2 stays in its safe hold. It lands a breath before the next tick. By the next night pass the strobe is off; one status lamp burns steady.
  Then Cargo 2 sends its event-recorder summary down the old channel. KEY UPDATE: NONE RECEIVED. HAIL RX · WRONG CHALLENGE ×31,000: five weeks of Earth's hails. HELLO RX · LATE · DISCARD ×350,000: the fleet's, eighteen ticks late at first, one at the last. At the bottom, one new line: HELLO RX · OK. Jai copies it into the logbook in capitals.
  The descent brief goes up four days out: clear air, straight in, the formation opening out to two minutes between ships. The prime zone is exactly where the maps said, with one more thing thirty kilometres past it. Kim packs a bag for the drive out: water, a torch, spare batteries, a marker.
  Diego holds a thumb up to the window, the old Earth-watch habit. Mars still fits behind it, for a few more nights: half rust, half dark, the dawn line straight down the middle. Just past that line, too small to see, a light is holding steady.
  The last nights before entry. Diego checks the water tanks that shielded the bunk all these weeks, by hand, the way you'd pat a horse. On the fleet channel Jai is doing the same on another ship. "Same time tomorrow?" Jai asks. "On the ground," Diego says. Jai sets an alarm for it.
  Phill uncaps the blue marker one last time and adds a second lighthouse to the drawing: small, standing on the ground, and beside it a figure holding a Sharpie. Then Phill folds it into a chest pocket with the marker.

FLIGHT DAY 42
  Jai writes to every name on the list from the first call, one more to the name that isn't there any more, and a last one to a loading bay at Starbase. Each will land eight minutes after it's sent. Jai sends them without a single pause: no ums, no gaps, no saving the good bit for last.
  On the last morning before the air, with a planet under the fleet again, Michelle plays past the four bars, all the way through, and this time on the channel. Six ships hear the fifth bar at last: it falls where they all had it rising, and lands on the last note of the galley chime. "That's the one," Michelle says.
  Entry interface in clear air. Four minutes of plasma blackout, seven ships silent in turn. At the landing burn one of Kim's engines lights late, a long half second, and the engine-out call is half out of Kim's mouth when it catches. The ground comes up exactly where the maps said.
  OLD FRIEND
  Arrival day. Second sol, thirty kilometres out, Kim driving, Phill on the battery: past half, and Cargo 2's knock on the rover radio no louder. There and back is more than the rover has. Kim keeps driving. Two kilometres out, Cargo 2 hears the rover and runs the pad crew's routine for a truck from the hangar: floods, hatch lamp, and the charge port on its landing leg, warm, its battery full since the strobe went off. Phill plugs in at four percent. The pack never takes a full charge again; the only rover runs at half range from now on. The first crate says, in Sharpie: "You'll thank me." To Starbase: "Landed. All eight."
