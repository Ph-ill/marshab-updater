FLIGHT DAY 01
  Jai calls home while the delay is still under a second. Someone tells a bad joke and Jai laughs on the beat, in real time, with everyone at the table. Afterwards Jai writes down who was there and what they ate, and folds the list into a flight-suit pocket.
  Phill and Jacques share the night watch over the fleet link. From low orbit Earth fills the window, its dark side strung with cities. Phill holds up a thumb, and the planet spills out around it on every side. They talk about home as if it were down the road. For a few more days, it is.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Phill, Jacques, Kim and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 02
  Michelle plays the same four bars on a travel guitar at the end of every watch, into the fleet channel, never the rest of the song. By the end of the week six ships are guessing at the fifth bar, six different ways.
  The CO₂ scrubber on Jacques's ship throws its fourth fault this week. Jacques fixes it, then labels it "temperamental" in permanent marker, under a label in Starbase handwriting that already says "be nice to it".
  Michelle and Diego watch the tankers come and go from their own windows, a few kilometres apart, and narrate each docking on the fleet channel like a sports commentary. By the third one they have a catchphrase, and by the fourth it has gone fleet-wide.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill and Jacques.

FLIGHT DAY 03
  The galley screen on Kim's ship takes a 2.3-gigabyte update overnight and restarts at breakfast: the logo, a progress bar, the three-note chime, all over before Kim has counted to eleven. By the end of the week the whole fleet is humming the chime.
  Phill and Kim share the exercise slot over video, two hours a day so their bones still work at Mars. Neither will hang up first. The sessions keep getting longer.
  Meanwhile Jacques, Phill and Jai kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 04
  Dorian plants the garden: six seed pods in a tray the size of a laptop, under a lamp the colour of a plum. Dorian labels each pod in tiny capitals and says good night to them on an open channel. Several people say good night back.
  For whoever at home looks after Diego, and nobody else: The airlock on Diego's ship won't cycle. One door sensor says "closed". The other says "ajar". The interlock won't move until they agree. The door is obviously closed, and Diego has the override code.
  Dorian stands the night watch for the whole fleet so the other six can sleep. Unasked. The log entry is two lines long and perfectly neat.
  Off the main thread, Jacques, Jai and Michelle had a full day.

FLIGHT DAY 05
  Jai reads the arrival manifest: habitat on Cargo 1, water on Cargo 3, the rover on Cargo 4. The written-off page runs longer than expected: a drill rig left in Earth orbit, a nitrogen pallet vented on the pad, Cargo 2 "lost on landing", a dead weather orbiter, a grey relay, "thruster stuck open".
  Around the fleet, Phill, Jacques, Jai and Michelle kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 06
  The last tanker undocks at dawn. Seven ships light their engines for the departure burn, and Jai feels weight come back for the first time since launch. From here the flight log keeps one page for every five days, and by the next page Jai can hide Earth behind a thumb.
  A replacement pump won't sit in its mount on Michelle's ship. Every part is in tolerance. Stacked together, they're 1.4 millimeters out. Michelle shims it with a washer from the spares kit and writes the stack-up on the bulkhead for the next person.
  Diego walks Kim through the manual landing abort over the link, step by step, until Kim can call it without the checklist. Neither of them mentions why it matters.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Jacques and Kim, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 07
  No word from home in time, so Diego chose: Find a third opinion. Diego pulls the lock's pressure trace as a tiebreaker. The door is sealed, and the "ajar" sensor is lying. Diego swaps it, the interlock is satisfied, and nothing was overridden.
  The ships hold formation a few hundred metres apart. Every fifth flight day they close up for a transfer window: a tethered line between hatches, a morning on oxygen first, paid for in RCS gas and everyone's time, so no one crosses for fun. Jai rides the line first and calls it "educational".
  Every seventh flight day the fleet eats together on video: seven tablets on seven galley tables, one meal at one time. Michelle is in charge of the toast. It's short, it isn't funny, and it lands.
  Fleet decision open for two flight days. Public affairs wants a fleet portrait: seven ships in one frame, with Earth behind them. That means closing to a careful hundred metres apart, and propellant to get there and back. The request uses the word "iconic" four times. [Fly the formation / Politely decline]
  Dorian and Phill keep a channel open as Earth and the Moon slide into the same frame, each at their own window on their own ship. For a long time neither says anything, and the link carries the hum of two sets of fans.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill, Jacques and Jai.

FLIGHT DAY 08
  The data drop brings Kim one file flagged personal, from the one person at home who never sends files.
  For whoever at home looks after Kim, and nobody else: Every cabin has a camera feed to the medical team on the ground; the airlock is the one place aboard without one. Reading it now means reading it in an airlock. Saving it means carrying it unread until a quiet moment that may never come.
  The hatch on Phill's ship sticks whenever the sunward side warms up. Phill works it out on the back of a checklist: the frame grows a millimeter in the sun and the seal doesn't. One new shim, and the door swings the way it did at Starbase.
  Diego inventories the spares locker by hand and finds a filter cartridge the manifest says was never loaded. Somebody at Starbase packed a margin and ticked the bag in Sharpie. Diego logs it, with a thank-you in the margin.
  Dorian waters the garden tray with a syringe, two millilitres per pod, and checks each one under the lamp. Nothing yet. Dorian logs the nothing as carefully as anything else.
  Meanwhile Phill and Michelle kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 09
  A tie, 4 votes in all. The crew broke it themselves: Politely decline. The fleet sends a polite no and a very good photo of a single ship. Public affairs replies with one word: "Noted."
  The weekly radiation summary puts Jacques's dosimeter at the top of the fleet's list, a little higher every week. The bunk on Jacques's ship sits against the thin side of the hull.
  For whoever at home looks after Jacques, and nobody else: Moving the bunk means telling the flight surgeon, who will want a meeting, and a meeting with the ground gets slower every week.
  Fleet decision open for two flight days. Water recovery on Michelle's ship is down to 93 percent from 98. Michelle wants to strip the processor now. Diego wants to run it degraded and fix it when things are calmer. [Strip it now / Run it degraded]
  For whoever at home looks after Jai, and nobody else: The attitude control on Jai's ship has a slow wobble, and Jai seems to be the only one who notices it. A small adjustment would probably settle it. Procedure says any change needs sign-off from the guidance team, and the guidance team is off shift until morning.
  Off the main thread, Kim, Phill and Jacques had a full day.

FLIGHT DAY 10
  Dorian and Phill stand the night watch together over the fleet link, each in a dark cabin. Earth is a bright dot now, small enough to cover with a thumbnail. They each make a coffee at the same minute, as if that counted as sharing one.
  Jacques wakes from a dream of rain on a car roof and lies still, trying to keep the sound. The fans hum. The ship ticks as it turns into the sun. By breakfast the rain has gone, and Jacques doesn't mention it to anyone.
  01:50. The cabin pressure on Phill's ship has started to fall. Slowly. Phill is the only one awake.
  Around the fleet, Phill, Jacques and Jai kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 11
  Kim went with your call: Save it. Kim saves it for a bad day. Every mission has one, and now Kim has something set aside for it.
  Decision closed: Run it degraded. Votes cast: 3. The fleet goes on water rationing. The complaints stay off the channel. Michelle logs the recovery rate every hour, which is its own kind of complaint.
  Behind a stowage panel on Jacques's ship, three ration packs that never made the manifest. Someone at Starbase wrote "you'll thank me" on the bag in Sharpie, next to a little drawing of a Starship with its fingers crossed.
  For whoever at home looks after Jacques, and nobody else: The three packs from behind the panel are the good rations, the ones the galley ran out of first. They aren't on any inventory.
  Phill spent four hours with an ultrasonic leak detector and a roll of tape. The culprit was a cable feedthrough whose seal shrank in the cold of the shaded side. It's resealed, the pressure holds, and the trend line is taped above the console like a fish someone caught.
  Fleet decision open for two flight days. Mission Control proposes moving the fleet's clocks two hours forward, to line up with the ground team's shifts. Kim is for it. Jai has seen this study before. [Change the clocks / Keep fleet time]
  For whoever at home looks after Michelle, and nobody else: Michelle finds a hairline crack in a bracket holding a stowage rack, at a corner the model said would carry no stress. A proper fix means opening a ticket with Earth. A zip-tie fix takes five minutes.
  Kim and Jai spend a whole shift arguing on the fleet channel about what plays during the next burn. Kim wants silence, so every noise means something. Jai wants anything with a chorus. They settle on one song, chosen by the ground team, sight unseen. It's worse than either.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Jacques, Phill, Jai and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 12
  Jacques went with your call: Tell the surgeon. The meeting runs on a small pause per question, and everyone learns to ask two at once. Jacques's bunk moves in beside the water tanks, and the reading drops by a third.
  Jai went with your call: Adjust it now. Jai makes the change and watches the wobble die over six cycles. The ground signs off in the morning with one word, "Nice", and one attachment: the procedure for next time.
  The galley screen on Phill's ship has started to sulk: after every restart it sits on its logo a little longer than it used to, then boots with a sad cloud icon and the word OFFLINE. Phill files a ticket titled "manners".
  A flare has gone off on the Sun, and its protons will reach the fleet in about forty minutes. Diego packs into the storm shelter between the water tanks with a sleeping bag and a deck of cards, and every crew in the fleet does the same, cards over video, until the dosimeter settles.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill and Jai.

FLIGHT DAY 13
  Decision closed: Keep fleet time. Votes cast: 3. The fleet keeps its clocks. The ground team adjusts instead, and sends a photo of the night shift's pizza as evidence.
  Fleet decision open for two flight days. Navigation shows the fleet drifting toward the edge of the arrival corridor. Michelle wants a small correction burn now. Diego wants to wait for the scheduled burn, which costs more but leaves time to check the solution three times. [Burn now / Wait and check]
  On the long shifts, Jacques keeps one small thing from home within reach of the console. The other six ships have stopped asking about it, and would notice if it went.
  Dorian records a message for a school group back home that's been following the fleet. The kids send back forty questions, mostly about the toilet. Dorian answers every one.
  Meanwhile Jai, Phill and Michelle kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 14
  Jacques went with your call: Hand them in. Jacques turns them in, bag and all. The galley has an unplanned surplus for the first time since launch, and the bag goes up on the galley wall.
  Michelle went with your call: Zip-tie it. Michelle lashes the rack in a pattern no design review would pass, and it takes every load the ship can throw at it. The anomaly report calls it "interim". It is not interim.
  After a long debate on the fleet channel, Kim and Jai get all seven ships to agree on which drawer the zip ties live in. It has taken the whole flight so far, and it may be the most important engineering decision made out here.
  Michelle and Dorian compare storm shelters on video and steal each other's best ideas: one more sleeping spot, a hook for the cards, and a sign, now on every ship, that reads "the Sun is not your friend".
  Just before midnight, 214 alarms hit Jai's console at once. One of them is real.
  Off the main thread, Phill, Jacques, Kim, Jai and Michelle had a full day.

FLIGHT DAY 15
  Decision closed: Wait and check. Votes cast: 5. The solution gets checked three times, then a fourth. The scheduled burn goes off on time and on target, and Diego lets the computer fly it.
  Phill's spare dish, parked on Mars for the arrival tests, still scans the old common channel, which nothing on Earth listens to. It logs a carrier too faint to lock. Folded at 10.96 seconds, 2.4 percent duty, it stands up like a fence post. "An alias," Phill says. "Something of ours."
  Jai found it at 01:30, buried on page nine: a coolant pump drawing too much current, minutes from tripping. Jai swapped to the backup before the primary died. By morning the alarm list had been cut from 214 to a page, and nobody is sure when Jai slept.
  Around the fleet, Jai and Michelle kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 16
  A voice note from the Starbase loading crew turns up in Phill's data drop, forty seconds long, recorded on a forklift by the sound of it. The one with the Sharpie: "Eat the good rations first. The bad ones keep." Phill checks the locker. It's true.

  Anyone can answer this one. One tap is enough. Diego needs the ultrasound and splint set from another ship's medical kit, and the next scheduled transfer window is days away. [Send the kit / Michelle brings it over]
  Jacques closes their eyes in the bunk and sees a white spark, then another. Cosmic rays, passing straight through the eye, fast enough to make light; cameras see them too, as single bright pixels. Jacques counts them like sheep and loses count somewhere in the twenties.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Phill, Jacques and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 17

[Send the data / Letters first]
  Jai and Jacques try to bake bread in the galley oven while the visit lasts. Without gravity it rises in every direction at once and comes out shaped like a lung. They eat all of it, warm, with the lights down, and agree not to describe it to anyone at home.
  It's Jai's birthday by the ground calendar, and Jacques is aboard for the transfer window. Jacques produces a candle that can't be lit, a cake that is a ration bar with a slice of dried mango on it, and a card signed by the whole fleet. Jai keeps the candle.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill, Kim, Michelle and Jai.

FLIGHT DAY 18

Phill wants to answer every burst with the standard handshake, signed with the old program key the fleet still carries. Michelle points out that every reply ties up the comms computer, and the letters from home queue behind a machine. [Answer every burst / Log it, keep going]

  Kim cleans up the receiver's noise floor for the weekly report and finds a faint ripple in it, at about nine hundredths of a hertz. A control loop hunting somewhere, most likely the coolant pump's: it's on the same bus. Kim notes it in the margin and moves on to the propellant tables.
  For whoever at home looks after Kim, and nobody else: An external camera on Kim's ship has iced over, and its heater can't shift it. Clearing it now means the next ship closing up for an unscheduled window, someone suited on the other end. No one would blame Kim for waiting for the planned EVA.
  Jacques teaches Jai a card game from home across a real galley table, not a screen. By the end of the shift Jai is winning, and the rules have been amended three times.
  Diego counts the medical kit against the manifest: sutures, splints, pain tablets in neat blister rows. Diego counts it once more and closes the lid gently.
  Meanwhile Jacques and Kim kept the lights on.

  Jai copies the list from the first call into the entry log, name by name, and reaches the end feeling it's short. It is: the person who held the phone up that night never made it onto the list, because they were holding the phone. Jai adds them at the top.
  Long table night. This time Dorian has the toast and gives it to the empty chair at each of the seven tables, the one where a second crew member would sit on a normal ship. It lands on a short delay on all six screens. Diego laughs on the delay, and it still counts.
  Around the fleet, Phill, Jacques, Kim, Jai and Michelle kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 36
  Phill takes the old-channel receiver log, slides it back by the light time from Mars, about twenty-five seconds, and lays it over the fast sequence. The carrier peaks on the exact frame the pixel lights. Every time. It isn't two things. Phill puts both traces on the galley screen, side by side.
  Alone, with the channel off, Michelle plays past the four bars for the first time since Starbase and gets the fifth one wrong. Then wrong again, differently. Michelle puts the guitar back in its bag. "Not yet," Michelle tells the empty cabin. "Entry morning."
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Phill, Jacques, Jai and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 37
  Mission Control answers the overlay: nothing near that pixel still has power. The nearest entry is the crash site catalogued for Cargo 2, kilometres off, written off after six weeks of ground hails and no acknowledgement. Crash sites don't blink. It goes on the next survey anyway.
  Rationed water was one squeeze too many: the last pod never came up. Dorian keeps watering the tray, two millilitres, on schedule, and packs the dry seed in a coat pocket for the ground. Seeds are patient. So, it turns out, is Dorian.
  The rest of the log belongs to Jacques and Jai.

FLIGHT DAY 38
  Diego takes the lighthouse drawing down for the entry checklist and stops. A relay would drag the knocks early and late by most of a tenth of a second each orbit, and never hide for half a day. These swing by a hundredth, once a day. Under the keeper, in blue: "Wrong lighthouse." Then: "Same keeper?"
  With Mars under three weeks out, the old-channel carrier is loud enough on the dish to hear without folding. Michelle pipes it into the cabin speaker: a click, a hiss, a long pause, a click. The four bars fit between the clicks. Michelle plays them once on the galley keyboard and doesn't explain.
  Meanwhile Michelle and Phill kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 39
  Phill works out how many times the old-channel carrier has knocked since the fleet first heard it: most of a million, and not one missed. "Somebody is very patient," Phill tells the fleet channel. On the ground, the key-rotation ticket has quietly been moved to after landing.
  Jacques times the old-channel carrier on the galley clock, the way you count between lightning and thunder. Everything about the fleet's distance from Mars has changed since the first night. The gap hasn't. Jacques stops counting before the number means anything.
  Off the main thread, Michelle, Phill, Jacques, Jai and Dorian had a full day.

FLIGHT DAY 40
  The first sharp pass over the backup site since before the cargo ships flew comes down. Past the prime zone, behind a crater rim, a ship stands on its legs, tilted, one solar wing half open, its dish jammed against its stop: Cargo 2. From straight above it was always two pixels and a shadow. In the night video its floodlights flare at power-up, die, flare again. Diego hums the galley chime; it fits the gap.
  Michelle pulls up the report that wrote Cargo 2 off: a page and a half. The grey relay's thruster stuck open on the burn to cover its landing pass. Last packet: X-band amplifier OVERTEMP. Six weeks of ground hails, no acknowledgement; an old-band carrier, attributed to relay tumble. Ticket closed. Michelle's own report runs forty pages by midnight. Page one: watchdog. Page two: what lives in RAM.
  The entry checklist has a line for "personal items stowed". Kim reads the last line of the letter once more, aloud, then stows it and ticks the box. Somewhere below, something has been writing home for two years and filing every answer as late.
  Phobos is a speck beside Mars now, crossing its face in under an hour. Phill watches it cross a whole world in the time it takes to eat a meal, and for the first time since Starbase wants weight more than sleep.
  Around the fleet, Phill, Kim, Jai, Michelle and Dorian kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 41
  04:10:07, fleet time: Cargo 2 knocks. Nothing on the fleet's schedule answers. An answer from Earth would land minutes after the knock gave up. The fleet's would take ten seconds. The knock lasts eleven. Mission Control asks the fleet to hold anyway: no one knows what a two-year-old fault tree does with its first good handshake, and seven ships are about to land beside it.
  The descent brief goes up four days out. The storm fills the prime zone rim to rim, so the plan retargets to the backup site and gains a line no one has had to write before: "radar for height, Cargo 2 for place". Kim reads it to the empty cabin, then runs the manual abort once, from memory.
  On the last log page before entry, Jacques holds a thumb up to the window, the old habit from the Earth watches. Since Starbase everything has fitted behind it. Mars still does, for a few more nights: rust at the edges, dark in the middle. Somewhere on the night side, too small to see, a light is keeping time.
  The last nights before entry. Jacques checks the water tanks that shielded the bunk all these weeks, by hand, the way you'd pat a horse. On the fleet channel Diego is doing the same on another ship. "Same time tomorrow?" Diego asks. "On the ground," Jacques says. Neither of them sleeps much.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Phill, Jacques, Jai and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 42
  Entry interface. Four minutes of plasma blackout, seven ships silent at once. At six kilometres the cameras go brown. Kim's ship slews in a gust the model never had, and the abort call sits on Kim's tongue, the one drilled over the link and run four days ago from memory. Then the radar finds the ground, and Kim doesn't call it.
  Jai writes to every name on the list from the first call, one more to the name that isn't there any more, and a last one to a loading bay at Starbase. Each will land ten minutes after it's sent. "No pauses this time," Jai says, and leaves them out.
  On the morning of entry, with a planet under the fleet again, Michelle plays the song all the way through on the fleet channel, past the four bars, for the first time since Starbase. Six ships hear the fifth bar at last. It isn't what any of them guessed. "That's the one," Michelle says.
  On the entry map Jai finds the crater the fleet named for the first person who ever wrote a Mars plan. Cargo 2 has been standing behind its rim the whole time. Somewhere on Earth, a committee is about to have a great deal more to argue about.
  Diego straps in for entry with the wrist that wouldn't bend, mostly bending now, and doesn't mention the "mostly" to the flight surgeon.
  Through the blackout Dorian's ship flies one thruster short, on the workaround written around the valve that night. Out the far side it rolls once the wrong way, slowly, a motion the model never had, and comes back. Dorian initials the log line, then the next, and breathes.
  Before strapping in, Diego folds the lighthouse drawing into a chest pocket with the blue marker. Overnight a second lighthouse has appeared on it, small, standing on the ground, in the same blue.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill, Jacques, Jai, Diego and Dorian.
  THROUGH THE DUST
  Arrival day. Retargeted to the backup site, as voted, the fleet goes down into the storm. The radar ranges on fifty metres of standing steel; out of the brown, every eleven seconds, a light, still asking. Seven ships land three kilometres off. The first suit out carries the hail box and answers from two hundred metres, and the event recorder comes down: two years of HELLO RX · REPLAY · DISCARD, every hello from home filed as late, then one new line: HELLO RX · OK. In the hold, the first crate says, in Sharpie: "You'll thank me." The first message off the surface goes to a loading bay at Starbase: "Landed. All eight."
