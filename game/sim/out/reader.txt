FLIGHT DAY 01
  Jai calls home while the delay is still under a second. Someone tells a bad joke and Jai laughs on the beat, in real time, with everyone at the table. Afterwards Jai writes down who was there and what they ate, and folds the list into a flight-suit pocket.
  Phill and Jacques share the night watch over the fleet link. From low orbit Earth fills the window, its dark side strung with cities. Phill holds up a thumb, and the planet spills out around it on every side. They talk about home as if it were down the road. For a few more days, it is.

FLIGHT DAY 02
  Michelle plays the same four bars on a travel guitar at the end of every watch, into the fleet channel, never the rest of the song. By the last top-off tanker six ships are guessing at the fifth bar, six different ways.
  The ships fly a few kilometres apart, one seat a ship, so no failure takes two people. The next ship reads back every critical command; votes are advice to the flight director. Every few days they close up on a tethered line, after prebreathe. Dorian has the first crossing and calls it "educational".
  Michelle and Diego watch the last top-off tankers come and go from their own windows, a few kilometres apart, and narrate each docking on the fleet channel like a sports commentary. By the third one they have a catchphrase, and by the fourth it has gone fleet-wide.

FLIGHT DAY 03
  The galley screen on Kim's ship takes a 2.3-gigabyte update overnight and restarts at breakfast: the logo, a progress bar, the three-note chime, all over before Kim has counted to eleven. Then it logs out again. By departure the whole fleet is humming the chime.
  Phill and Jacques share the exercise slot over video, two hours a day so their bones still work at Mars. Whoever hangs up first owes the other a dessert. The sessions keep getting longer.
  Jai answered mail from home: weather, a dog, a fence that needs mending.

FLIGHT DAY 04
  Dorian plants the garden: six seed pods in a tray the size of a laptop, under a lamp the colour of a plum. Dorian labels each pod in tiny capitals and says good night to them on an open channel. Several people say good night back.
  Kim stands the night watch for the whole fleet so the other six can sleep. Unasked. The watch report is two lines long and perfectly neat.

FLIGHT DAY 05
  Jai reads the manifest like a shopping list: water on Cargo 3, rover on Cargo 4. Write-offs: a grey relay, "thruster stuck open"; the last window's hopper, "tipped, beacon intermittent"; Cargo 2, drill spares, "lost on landing, scar on a survey frame"; socks, "reason unknown".
  Phill, Jacques and Michelle cleaned galley filters and found a missing spoon.

FLIGHT DAY 06
  The last tanker undocks at dawn. Seven ships light their engines for the departure burn, and Diego feels weight come back for the first time since launch. From here the flight log keeps one page for every five days, and by the next page Diego can hide Earth behind a thumb.
  A replacement pump won't sit in its mount on Jai's ship. Every part is in tolerance. Stacked together, they're 1.4 millimeters out. Jai shims it with a washer from the spares kit and writes the stack-up on the bulkhead for the next person.
  Diego walks Kim through taking the landing by hand over the link: hold, pick a new spot, commit. Step by step, until Kim can call it without the checklist. Diego logs it as training. It is.
  Jacques counted spare fuses twice and got the same numbers both times.

FLIGHT DAY 07
  Once a week the fleet eats together on video: seven tablets on seven galley tables, one meal at one time. Michelle is in charge of the toast. It's short, it isn't a joke, and it lands.
  A flare on the Sun: its protons will reach the fleet in about forty minutes. Dorian packs into the storm shelter between the water tanks with a deck of cards, and every crew in the fleet does the same, cards over video, until the dosimeter settles.
  Private, for whoever looks after Jacques: An external camera on Jacques's ship has iced over, and its heater can't shift it. Clearing it now means the next ship closing up for an unscheduled window, someone suited on the other end. No one would blame Jacques for waiting.
  Diego and Dorian keep a channel open, each at their own porthole, as Earth and the Moon draw together night by night. For a long time the link carries only the hum of two sets of fans. At the shift change Dorian sends one photo: two crescents, one blue, one grey.
  Something hits Phill's ship hard enough to tick through the whole hull. Every pressure reading is green. Phill doesn't trust green.

FLIGHT DAY 08
  Phill's morning EVA, Michelle suited at the next ship's hatch, found a fingernail crater in a heat-shield tile, short of the bondline. The entry team clears it on one condition: Phill's ship flies entry a few tenths of a degree steeper, for a sharper peak and a shorter soak.
  The data drop brings Jacques one file flagged personal, from the one person at home who never sends files.
  Private, for whoever looks after Jacques: The airlock is the one place aboard with no camera feed to the ground, so reading it now means reading it there. Saving it means carrying it unread until a quiet moment that may never come.
  Private, for whoever looks after Kim: Kim finds a hairline crack in a bracket holding a stowage rack, at a corner the model said would carry no stress. A proper fix means opening a ticket with Earth. A zip-tie fix takes five minutes.
  Dorian waters the garden tray with a syringe, two millilitres per pod, and checks each one under the lamp. Nothing yet. Dorian writes it down: "Nothing yet. Good, even nothing." Then tells the pods so.
  Jai records a message for a school group back home that's been following the fleet. The kids send back forty questions, mostly about the toilet. Jai answers every one.

FLIGHT DAY 09
  The weekly radiation summary puts Diego's dosimeter at the top of the fleet's list, higher every week; the bunk sits on bare hull. The dose model issues Diego a "career remaining" figure. The form has a box for questions. It is very small.
  Private, for whoever looks after Diego: Moving the bunk means telling the flight surgeon, who will want a meeting, and a meeting with the ground gets slower every week.
  Private, for whoever looks after Michelle: The attitude control on Michelle's ship has a slow wobble only Michelle seems to notice. A switch to the alternate gain set would probably settle it. Procedure says the guidance team signs off any switch, and the console on shift wants the responsible engineer's signature.
  Kim and Jai compare storm shelters on video and steal each other's best ideas: one more sleeping spot, a hook for the cards, and a sign, now on every ship, that reads "the Sun is not your friend".
  Phill and Jacques re-torqued hatch hinges to spec and wrote down the numbers.

FLIGHT DAY 10
  Jacques went with your call: Wait for the planned EVA. Jacques waits. The camera stays blind for a few days, which the risk assessment calls acceptable. Jacques has read all of it, appendix included.
  With the cabin dark and the Sun behind the hull, Jacques sees the zodiacal light with no air in the way: a long faint cone of comet dust, lit from behind. From home it is a smudge at dusk. Out here it is a road. Jacques turns the screen brightness down to keep it.
  Michelle and Dorian stand the night watch together over the fleet link, each in a dark cabin. Earth is a thin bright crescent now, low beside the Sun's glare, less than half the width of the Moon from home. They each make a coffee at the same minute, as if that counted as sharing one.

FLIGHT DAY 11
  Jacques went with your call: Read it now. Jacques reads it in the airlock and comes out ten minutes later without saying what it said. That evening Jacques calls the other ships one by one, about nothing in particular.
  No word from home in time, so Kim chose: Zip-tie it. Kim lashes the rack in a pattern no design review would pass, and it takes every load the ship can throw at it. The anomaly report calls it "interim". It is not interim.
  Behind a stowage panel on Kim's ship, three ration packs that never made the manifest. Someone at Starbase wrote "you'll thank me" on the bag in Sharpie, next to a little drawing of a Starship with its fingers crossed.
  Private, for whoever looks after Kim: The three packs from behind the panel are the good rations, the ones the galley ran out of first. They aren't on any inventory.
  Methane boil-off on Phill's ship runs high while it flies broadside to feed the solar wings. Phill proposes nose-to-Sun, wings canted, so the ship's own shadow falls down the tanks. The ground runs the power numbers and agrees; boil-off drops by a third, fleet-wide.
  Fleet vote: Mission Control proposes moving the fleet's clocks two hours forward, to line up with the ground team's shifts. Jai is for it. Phill has seen this study before. [Change the clocks / Keep fleet time]
  Private, for whoever looks after Jai: The airlock on Jai's ship won't cycle. One door sensor says "closed". The other says "ajar". The interlock won't move until they agree. The door is obviously closed, and Jai has the override code.

FLIGHT DAY 12
  No word from home in time, so Diego chose: Keep it quiet. Diego notes it privately and moves the sleeping bag to the other wall. It isn't nothing. It isn't a meeting either.
  No word from home in time, so Michelle chose: Adjust it now. Michelle selects the alternate set and watches the wobble die over six cycles. The ground signs off in the morning, after a non-conformance report and a long call, and adds a second signature line for next time.
  Crossing window. The next ship's hatch takes a code that goes stale in a fifth of a second. The procedure still says to read them up via the ground. Dorian does, once: CODE STALE. Ship to ship it opens first time, and Dorian files a correction to page two.
  The CO₂ scrubber on Michelle's ship throws its fourth fault since the hatch closed on the pad. Michelle fixes it, then labels it "temperamental" in permanent marker, under a label in Starbase handwriting that already says "be nice to it".
  Diego's wristwatch is still on home time. At fleet midnight Diego glances at it and tells the net, "Breakfast at home." The answer comes back the same, from a different ship each time: "Enjoy."
  Jacques and Kim spend a whole shift arguing on the fleet channel about what plays during the next burn. Jacques wants silence, so every noise means something. Kim wants anything with a chorus. They settle on one song, chosen by the ground team, sight unseen. It's worse than either.
  Jai ran comms self-tests and filed clean sheets.
  Mission Control misses a scheduled check-in. Then the next one. Jacques sends a third hail and starts the clock: twice the light time, plus a minute for someone to put a coffee down.

FLIGHT DAY 13
  With the ballots in, the fleet's advice: Keep fleet time. The fleet keeps its clocks. The ground team adjusts instead, and sends a photo of the night shift's pizza.
  Eight hours of nothing, the one-page checklist open on every console. Before breakfast the reply arrives: a ground station went down in a storm at home, and the backup had to wait for Earth to turn. The message ends: "All good here. How are you?" Jacques starts with the weather.
  The galley screen on Michelle's ship has started to sulk: after every restart it sits on its logo longer than it used to, then boots with a sad cloud icon and the word OFFLINE. Michelle files a ticket titled "manners".
  During Diego's scheduled EVA a tether snags on a bracket and wrenches Diego's arm, with the next ship closed up and a suited crewmate clipped in at its hatch. Diego frees it, works back along the line hand over hand, and comes inside with a wrist that won't bend.
  Anyone can answer; one tap is enough. Diego needs the ultrasound probe from another ship's medical kit, and the next scheduled crossing window is days away. [Send the kit / Dorian brings it over]
  Fleet vote: Navigation shows the fleet drifting toward the edge of the arrival corridor. Jai wants a small correction burn now. Phill wants to wait for the scheduled burn, which costs more but leaves time to check the solution three times. [Burn now / Wait and check]
  Phill wakes from a dream of rain on a car roof and lies still, trying to keep the sound. The fans hum. The ship ticks as it turns into the sun. By breakfast the rain has gone. Phill writes "rain, car roof" in the log margin, in case it comes back.
  Dorian inventories the spares locker by hand and finds a scrubber cartridge the manifest says was never loaded. Somebody at Starbase packed a margin and ticked the bag in marker. Dorian logs it, with a thank-you in the margin.

FLIGHT DAY 14
  Kim went with your call: Hand them in. Kim turns them in, bag and all. The galley has an unplanned surplus for the first time since launch.
  Jai went with your call: Use the override. Jai reads the override to the next ship, hears it back, and starts the cycle; it stalls at ten kilopascals on a slow leak. Both sensors had a point: the door was closed, and a seal had crept a millimetre. Jai reseats it and opens a ticket.
  Help is on the way: Send the kit. The kit rides the line across alone. Diego's wrist is scanned and splinted by lunch, and the anomaly report runs to four pages.
  Kim cleans up the receiver's noise floor for the weekly report and finds a faint ripple in it, at about nine hundredths of a hertz. A control loop hunting somewhere, most likely the coolant pump's: it's on the same bus. "Pump's hunting," Kim tells the ground, and books it a service slot.
  After a long debate on the fleet channel, Dorian and Phill get all seven ships to agree on which drawer the zip ties live in. It has taken the whole flight so far, and it may be the most important engineering decision made out here.

FLIGHT DAY 15
  With the ballots in, the fleet's advice: Wait and check. The solution gets checked three times, then a fourth. The scheduled burn goes off on time and on target, and Phill lets the computer fly it.
  Kim's arrival-test dish scans the old common channel toward Mars; no one has listened there since the relay was written off. A week of integration shows a faint carrier. Folded at 10.96 seconds, 2.4 percent duty, it stands up like a fence post. "An alias, probably," Kim says.
  Phill, Jai and Michelle stripped and re-taped frayed cable runs.

FLIGHT DAY 16
  A voice note from the Starbase loading crew reaches Michelle, a forklift beeping behind it. The one with the Sharpie: "Eat the good rations first. The bad ones keep. And say hello to the hardware for us. It sulks if no one does." Michelle checks the galley log: the good rations went first, fleet-wide. True.
  Diego spots a glint keeping pace off the starboard side, catching the sun every few seconds. Diego notes its bearing hourly. By the fourth hour it's plainly tumbling, each flash a face of ice from the last waste-water dump, a little smaller each time. The notes are neat enough to frame.
  02:14. Phill's ship starts to roll with nothing in the command log. The control loop fires a thruster to stop it. Then another. Phill is already at the console.

FLIGHT DAY 17
  Fourteen minutes of the control loop fighting a roll nothing had commanded, pulse after pulse on one axis. Phill let the control loop fight it and watched the pulses, counting. Every one traced to a single valve that reported "closed" while it leaked. It's isolated now, and that axis flies one thruster short for the rest of the voyage.
  Phill looks up the grey relay. Its thruster stuck open before the cargo landings; in its last week on the books, three power-only passes caught a pulse every 10.96 seconds, 2.4 percent of the time; the ground read it as a spin. Nothing else used the channel. Phill sketches a lighthouse in blue.
  Jacques reads the fleet's dose charts like the news, notices Diego's numbers, and calls. Jacques's dosimeter has the newer calibration, and the two of them redo Diego's week with it. That night Diego moves the bunk in beside the water tanks, and Jacques stays on the line until it's done.
  The CO₂ scrubber on Michelle's ship fails outright. With one person in a cabin that size, the CO₂ only climbs about a twentieth of a percent a day: not an emergency yet, which is the best time to fix one. Michelle has the backup running in nine minutes, and it is already complaining.
  Anyone can answer; one tap is enough. Michelle's ship is breathing on its backup scrubber. It needs a spare cartridge before the backup gives out. [Send a cartridge / Jai brings it over]
  Fleet vote: Water recovery on Jacques's ship is down to 93 percent from 98. Jacques wants to strip the processor now. Jai wants to run it degraded and fix it when things are calmer. [Strip it now / Run it degraded]
  Diego and Phill try to bake bread in the galley oven. Without gravity it rises in every direction at once and comes out shaped like a lung. They eat all of it, warm, with the lights down, and agree not to describe it to anyone at home.
  It's Jacques's birthday by the ground calendar, and Jai happens to be aboard. Jai produces a candle that can't be lit, a cake that is a ration bar with a slice of dried mango on it, and a card signed by the whole fleet. Jacques keeps the candle.

FLIGHT DAY 18
  Help is on the way: Send a cartridge. A spare cartridge rides the line across in an unscheduled crossing. Michelle's ship is breathing easy by evening.
  The delay has turned calls into letters. Jai records one for home, talking straight through the gaps, deletes it, tries again with pauses, which is worse, then goes at speed: "Hi, it's me, ship's fine, I'm fine, the fans hum in a key now, here's the list," and that one goes.
  The hatch on Kim's ship sticks whenever the sunward side warms up. Kim works it out on the back of a checklist: the frame grows a millimeter in the sun and the seal doesn't. One new shim, and the door swings the way it did at Starbase.
  Movie night with Diego, on a tablet stuck to the ceiling: Phill picked a film set on a submarine. Halfway through, the hull ticks as the ship turns out of the sun. Phill pauses it, and the two of them listen until it stops. Then Phill puts on something with a sky in it.
  Jacques swapped scrubber cartridges two days early, because the trend lines said so.

FLIGHT DAY 19
  With the ballots in, the fleet's advice: Run it degraded. Jacques's ship goes on water rationing, and the other six trim theirs a notch so there's a bag to send at the next window. Jacques writes down the recovery rate every hour; it slips a tenth at a time.
  On the corridor burn, the attitude trace from Michelle's ship is a flat line, and every other ship shows the wobble Michelle found. After the ground runs the fix through the simulator twice, Michelle's numbers go out to all seven, the report stapled to them.
  Four of the six pods have gone soft and grey: drowned. The schedule came from a book written for windowsills, where water drains away. Up here it just sits. Dorian lifts them out with tweezers. "Wrong book," Dorian tells the tray. "Let's go again." Half a dose, now, for the other two.
  The galley pump fails mid-breakfast. Kim lays the parts out on a towel in order, rebuilds it, and the washer that was missing at the factory turns up in the old housing.
  Fleet vote: A gamma-ray burst goes off far past the galaxy, and every ship's X-ray monitor catches it. Timed against detectors near Earth, the raw buffers could pin the source, but pulling them home bumps a day of mail. Dorian wants the data sent. Jacques wants the letters. [Send the data / Letters first]
  Michelle shuts both eyes in the bunk and sees a white spark, then another. Cosmic rays, passing straight through the eye, fast enough to make light; cameras see them too, as single bright pixels. Michelle counts them like sheep and loses count somewhere in the twenties.

FLIGHT DAY 20
  Kim asks Diego to point a second dish at the carrier. The bursts land on both in the same millisecond and slide against fleet time by over a second and a half a day, the fleet's closing rate on Mars. Hour to hour, the residuals are flat to a few milliseconds. "Booked again. Out," Kim logs.
  Fleet vote: If it's the grey relay, it may still know a standard hello. Kim wants clearance to answer every burst with one, signed with the old program key the fleet carries as a relay fallback: a courtesy, and a test. Diego points out that every reply ties up the comms computer. [Answer every burst / File it, keep going]
  Jai clips a new blank postcard inside the flight-plan binder every page, a different city each time, nothing on the back. The fleet guesses where from. Jai has confirmed one, and won't say which.

FLIGHT DAY 21
  With the ballots in, the fleet's advice: Letters first. Ground telescopes get the burst anyway, less sharply. Jacques reads every letter, and doesn't pretend it was an easy trade.
  On the night Dorian finally asks about the four bars, Michelle plays them again, all four, and stops. "Planet first." Dorian says that's a deal, and starts humming a made-up fifth bar on purpose.
  Jai goes to the window to find Earth and has to look for a while. It's a point now, bluish, and no longer anywhere you could point to a house. Jai photographs it beside the window frame's bolt heads, for scale.
  Dorian counts the medical kit against the manifest: sutures, splints, pain tablets in neat blister rows. Dorian closes the lid on the count.
  Phill cleaned galley filters and found a missing spoon.
  01:50. The cabin pressure on Jacques's ship is falling, a tenth of a kilopascal an hour. Jacques is awake to see it first, a minute before the fleet's night watch.

FLIGHT DAY 22
  Split down the middle, 2 votes. The flight director takes the crew's lean: File it, keep going. The carrier goes into the anomaly file under "later". Kim clips the folded trace inside the binder, just in case. Kim writes a dissent, signed; Diego countersigns it.
  Jacques spent four hours panel by panel with a leak detector, suit staged at the hatch. The culprit was a cable feedthrough whose seal shrank in the shaded-side cold. The trend goes in the logbook like a fish someone caught; under it, the reserve line, which won't recover before Mars.
  The degraded water processor on Jacques's ship quits at 04:00 on a crossing day. Jacques drinks from the reserve bag through a morning of Jai's prebreathe while the next ship closes up. By afternoon Jai is across with the spare valve; water flows by dinner. It tops out at ninety-five percent, for good.
  Through the long lens Earth and the Moon share one frame now, close as two buttons on a coat. Phill sends a week of them home as a flipbook: "You are here. Both of you."
  The protein-crystal experiment in Kim's locker, a university lab's two years of work, cooks overnight when its freezer fan seizes. Kim reads the temperature trace, then calls the lab, who ask for photos anyway. There is no second batch, and no second flight for it.

FLIGHT DAY 23
  Diego kills every cabin light, shades the Sun-side window and waits twenty minutes for night eyes. The Milky Way comes up with the dark lanes in it, the ones no backyard ever shows. Diego names the ones in the book, then starts on the rest.
  Dorian spends a slow day re-stowing the whole ship to the arrival plan, locker by locker. When the others get to theirs, Dorian's list is already on the fleet channel, with tips.

FLIGHT DAY 24
  Jai plots a week of the old-channel carrier for the anomaly file. It isn't steady: there for part of each day, gone for the rest, and back a little later each morning than the last. It goes in the anomaly file, behind the dust forecast that's due first.
  Every attitude move on Phill's ship now takes a beat longer, routed around the valve that was isolated that night. The ground's thruster-select table has a new row with Phill's initials. Phill has stopped noticing, mostly, except at 02:00.
  Through the shade filter the Sun is a coin held further off each week. The solar arrays say it first, a percent or two less on every page. Dorian turns the cabin heater up a notch, and caps the filter on a star that used to be a sky.
  Fleet vote: Halfway, by the flight plan's clock. Michelle wants to break out the good rations and mark it properly. Kim would rather not count yet. [Mark it / Let it pass]
  Jacques swept cabins with leak detectors and found nothing, which is the point.

FLIGHT DAY 25
  Split down the middle, 2 votes. The flight director takes the crew's lean: Let it pass. Halfway passes without a word on the fleet channel. On every ship, at the same minute, someone looks at the clock anyway.
  Folding a week of noise, Kim finds half a frame of housekeeping on the old channel, cut off partway. Its period hasn't moved by a millisecond in a day. One field is a counter that hasn't moved since the scrap before. Whatever it counts, nothing has given it one.
  The ground can't run the grey relay forward: its orbit data is two years stale. The ground sends the last good ephemeris anyway, and an apology. Phill slides the lighthouse sketch into the flight-plan binder.
  The magnetometer on Michelle's ship twitches for six hours: the fleet is crossing the ion tail of a comet that last came this way before anyone aboard was born. There's nothing to see. Michelle sits by the window anyway, with the trace scrolling on a tablet.
  The school group writes again: they've built Mars in the classroom out of papier-mâché and want to know if it's the right red. Jai sends back a photo through the window, unprocessed, and a note: "Close. A bit more butterscotch."

FLIGHT DAY 26
  Jacques has read the letter so often that the fold has worn through. Jacques mends it along the crease and keeps it in the flight-suit pocket over the heart, which the checklist calls the "left chest pocket".
  Another flare, another forty minutes. Jacques is in the shelter between the water tanks in six, with the cards already dealt for seven screens. By now everyone knows which deck cheats at what, and the dosimeter has stopped being the most interesting number in the room.
  A dried leaf in a plastic sleeve rides in Jacques's thigh pocket, picked up on the walk to the pad. Every few days Jacques checks it and reports to the net: "Still green on one side." The ground files it as a status.

FLIGHT DAY 27
  A message from home for Jai: a retirement party, already over, for the neighbour who always brought the folding chairs, a name from the first call's list. The house is sold; they're moving to the sea. Someone saved Jai a chair. Jai watches the recording to the end and asks for the next watch.
  Under the last tray in the spares locker, Dorian finds a roll of tape that isn't on any list, and under it a laminated card in a bag: an old handshake procedure, headed in the same Sharpie hand: "Old manners, for old hardware." Beside it, the little ship again, fingers crossed.
  Phill and Michelle wiped down windows and watched Mars not move.

FLIGHT DAY 28
  A batch of photos from the Starbase loading crew reaches Kim: the bay, the cranes, a forklift with a name, on charge at the service port at the foot of a cargo ship's leg. The one with the Sharpie signs off: "Seven of you. Write when you land, every one." Kim clips the forklift inside the logbook.
  The flight surgeon calls it the third-quarter effect. On the video link Dorian and Phill find themselves on the same checklist page at the same minute, both stuck on step four. Dorian reads the steps aloud, the way they would on a pass, and Phill answers each one. They file a ticket: the page is too long.
  Fleet vote: The backup-site maps, stitched from imagery older than the cargo landings (the sharpest camera at Mars rode on the grey relay), show an unnamed crater small enough for the fleet to name. Michelle wants to name it after the first person who ever wrote a Mars mission plan. Diego wants somewhere at home. [Honor the planners / Name it for home]
  02:10. Michelle's star tracker drops out of lock: one star too many in the field, and the next frame doesn't agree with the first. Attitude hands over to the backup tracker, which has never been asked to fly alone.

FLIGHT DAY 29
  Six hours on the backup tracker, Michelle checking stars by hand. By morning the extra one has a name: a launch cover plate shed at departure, on nearly the fleet's orbit, gaining fifteen metres an hour. Tonight it slides past forty kilometres off the bow, and Starbase starts counting its cover plates.
  The latest navigation fix comes in: dead centre of the arrival corridor. The checked burn cost more than the quick one would have, and Diego runs the propellant table again for the margin. It still says yes. Diego stops running it.

FLIGHT DAY 30
  With the ballots in, the fleet's advice: Name it for home. The crater gets the name of a small town on Earth that most of Earth has never heard of. Within a day, the town's newspaper has a front page about it.
  Dorian pulls an old survey orbiter's low-light night frames of the landing region. On dark ground near the edge of one frame, one pixel is lit. On the next frame it isn't. A cosmic-ray hit on the sensor, like the hundred before it. Dorian plots it anyway.
  The stowage rack on Kim's ship has carried its zip ties through every burn since. Before arrival Kim checks them all, adds one more for luck, and updates the anomaly report: "still interim".
  Dorian pulls the morning weather from the Mars orbiters: a dust storm, building over the landing zone. On the map it's a brown thumbprint. Dorian lays a ruler across it, then lays the ruler across the backup site.
  Jacques, Phill, Jai and Michelle walked hulls with the camera and found them whole.

FLIGHT DAY 31
  Since the fleet kept its own clock, the ground's handover lands in the fleet's first coffee. Kim knows them all by name now, and they know Kim's coffee order, which is no use to anyone across this much space.
  The surface team sends the landing-site plan, and Michelle reads it out. Water first: Cargo 3's ice drill made it down with one bit, rated for a season. The engineer who built it adds a line at the bottom: "Be gentle with it."
  Long table night. Phill has the toast and gives it to the empty chair at each of the seven tables, the one where a second crew member would sit on a normal ship. It lands on every screen at once, the one conversation out here with no delay in it. Jai laughs on the beat, and it counts double.

FLIGHT DAY 32
  Dorian goes through every night frame of that patch this month. Every pass, on different rows of the sensor, the lit pixel lands on the same spot of ground. Dorian sends the list round. The ground checks its list: nearest on file is the hopper, tipped on its pad, beacon still coming and going.
  Fleet vote: Dorian wants to ask the ground to task an orbiter for a fast sequence over the lit pixel, at the cost of the fleet's next deep-space pass: every letter from home waits. Phill would leave it for the people who'll be down there with time to look. [Ask for the sequence / Leave it for the ground]
  02:40. The intercom on Jai's ship opens and plays four seconds of a kitchen somewhere: a kettle, a radio, someone laughing. The intercom's clock reads tomorrow.

FLIGHT DAY 33
  With the ballots in, the fleet's advice: Leave it for the ground. The pixel goes into the anomaly file, under a heading Phill writes in capitals. The letters from home arrive on time, all of them.
  Jai sat up till dawn with the intercom logs: a message from home, recorded the night before launch and queued for this day. Last week's update reset its clock, so it fired in the night and cut out when the buffer ran dry. The rest plays at breakfast. The first minute is gone for good.
  The entry mass budget wants the tank beside Diego's bunk pumped down. Diego argues to keep it and runs the dose numbers to prove it. With this little cruise left, they don't. "Fine," Diego tells the ground. "Pump it." Then, because someone once checked on Diego: "Thanks for checking."
  Earth is five light-minutes away. Diego asks the ground a yes-or-no question and makes a whole cup of tea before the yes arrives. Conversations have become letters; letters have become weather.
  Kim, Jacques and Michelle chased rattles behind panels and found pens.

FLIGHT DAY 34
  The new weather frames come down: the dust storm over the landing zone has doubled overnight, and the brown thumbprint now covers the zone with room to spare. On the shared map, the ruler line no longer reaches clear ground.
  Fleet vote: The dust storm over the landing zone is still growing, and the flight director wants the crew's recommendation. Jacques wants to hold the arrival plan and trust the margins. Kim wants to skim the atmosphere into a long parking orbit and wait it out, at a real cost in food. [Hold the plan / Long orbit, wait]

  THROUGH THE DUST
