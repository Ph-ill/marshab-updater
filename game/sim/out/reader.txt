FLIGHT DAY 01
  Jai calls home while the delay is still under a second. Someone tells a bad joke and Jai laughs on the beat, in real time, with everyone at the table. Afterwards Jai writes down who was there and what they ate, and folds the list into a flight-suit pocket.
  Phill and Jacques share the night watch over the fleet link. From low orbit Earth fills the window, its dark side strung with cities. Phill holds up a thumb, and the planet spills out around it on every side. They talk about home as if it were down the road. For a few more days, it is.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Phill, Jacques, Kim and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 02
  Michelle plays the same four bars on a travel guitar at the end of every watch, into the fleet channel, never the rest of the song. By the end of the week six ships are guessing at the fifth bar, six different ways.
  The CO₂ scrubber on Jacques's ship throws its fourth fault this week. Jacques fixes it, then labels it "temperamental" in permanent marker, under a label in Starbase handwriting that already says "be nice to it".
  Michelle and Diego watch the tankers come and go from their own windows, a few kilometres apart, and narrate each docking on the fleet channel like a sports commentary. By the third one they have a catchphrase, and by the fourth it has gone fleet-wide.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill and Jacques.

FLIGHT DAY 03
  The galley screen on Kim's ship takes a 2.3-gigabyte update overnight and restarts at breakfast: the cabin lights blink once for the self-test, the logo, the three-note chime, all over before Kim has counted to eleven. By the end of the week the whole fleet is humming the chime.
  Phill and Kim share the exercise slot over video, two hours a day so their bones still work at Mars. Neither will hang up first. The sessions keep getting longer.
  Meanwhile Jacques, Phill and Jai kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 04
  Dorian plants the garden: six seed pods in a tray the size of a laptop, under a lamp the colour of a plum. Dorian labels each pod in tiny capitals and says good night to them on an open channel. Several people say good night back.
  For whoever at home looks after Diego, and nobody else: The airlock on Diego's ship won't cycle. One door sensor says "closed". The other says "ajar". The interlock won't move until they agree. The door is obviously closed, and Diego has the override code.
  Dorian stands the night watch for the whole fleet so the other six can sleep. Nobody asked. The log entry is two lines long and perfectly neat.
  Off the main thread, Jacques, Jai and Michelle had a full day.

FLIGHT DAY 05
  Jai reads the arrival manifest while the last tanker of a month of refuelling docks. Habitat on Cargo 1, water on Cargo 3, the rover on Cargo 4, which sends the weather. Cargo 2 gets one line, "lost on landing, written off", and a coarse thumbnail from well outside the zone: a smudge and a streak.
  Around the fleet, Phill, Jacques, Jai and Michelle kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 06
  The last tanker undocks at dawn. Seven ships light their engines for the departure burn, and Jai feels weight come back for the first time since launch. When the engines stop, Earth is already shrinking, and by the next watch Jai can hide it behind a thumb.
  A replacement pump won't sit in its mount on Michelle's ship. Every part is in tolerance. Stacked together, they're 1.4 millimeters out. Michelle shims it with a washer from the spares kit and writes the stack-up on the bulkhead for the next person.
  Diego walks Kim through the backup docking procedure over the link, step by step, until Kim can do it without the checklist. Neither of them mentions why it matters.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Jacques and Kim, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 07
  Nobody at home weighed in, so Diego chose: Find a third opinion. Diego pulls the lock's pressure trace as a tiebreaker. The door is sealed, and the "ajar" sensor is lying. Diego swaps it, the interlock is satisfied, and nothing was overridden.
  The ships hold formation a few hundred metres apart. Every fifth day they close up for a transfer window: a tethered line between hatches, scheduled and paid for in propellant, so nobody crosses for fun. An empty ship flies itself. Jai rides it first and calls the middle "educational".
  Every seventh flight day the fleet eats together on video: seven tablets on seven galley tables, one meal at one time. Michelle is in charge of the toast. It's short, it isn't funny, and it lands.
  Fleet decision open for 48 hours. Public affairs wants a fleet portrait: seven ships in one frame, with Earth behind them. That means closing to portrait distance, tens of metres apart, and propellant to get there and back. The request uses the word "iconic" four times. [Fly the formation / Politely decline]
  Dorian and Phill keep a channel open as Earth and the Moon slide into the same frame, each at their own window on their own ship. For a long time neither says anything, and the link carries the hum of two sets of fans.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill, Jacques and Jai.

FLIGHT DAY 08
  The data drop brings Kim one file flagged personal, from the one person at home who never sends files.
  For whoever at home looks after Kim, and nobody else: Every cabin has a camera feed to the medical team on the ground; the airlock is the one place aboard without one. Reading it now means reading it in an airlock. Saving it means carrying it unread until a quiet moment that may never come.
  The hatch on Phill's ship sticks whenever the sunward side warms up. Phill works it out on the back of a checklist: the frame grows a millimeter in the sun and the seal doesn't. One new shim, and the door swings the way it did at Starbase.
  Diego inventories the spares locker by hand and finds a filter cartridge the manifest says was never loaded. Somebody at Starbase packed a margin and ticked the bag in Sharpie. Diego logs it and says a quiet thank-you to whoever it was.
  Dorian waters the garden tray with a syringe, two millilitres per pod, and checks each one under the lamp. Nothing yet. Dorian logs the nothing as carefully as anything else.
  Meanwhile Phill and Michelle kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 09
  A tie, 4 votes in all. The crew broke it themselves: Politely decline. The fleet sends a polite no and a very good photo of a single ship. Public affairs replies with one word: "Noted."
  The weekly radiation summary puts Jacques's dosimeter at the top of the fleet's list, a little higher every week. The bunk on Jacques's ship sits against the thin side of the hull.
  For whoever at home looks after Jacques, and nobody else: Moving the bunk means telling the flight surgeon, who will want a meeting, and a meeting with the ground gets slower every week.
  Fleet decision open for 48 hours. Water recovery on Michelle's ship is down to 93 percent from 98. Michelle wants to strip the processor now. Diego wants to run it degraded and fix it when things are calmer. [Strip it now / Run it degraded]
  For whoever at home looks after Jai, and nobody else: The attitude control on Jai's ship has a slow wobble, and Jai seems to be the only one who notices it. A small adjustment would probably settle it. Procedure says any change needs ground sign-off, and the ground is asleep.
  Off the main thread, Kim, Phill and Jacques had a full day.

FLIGHT DAY 10
  Dorian and Phill stand the night watch together over the fleet link, each in a dark cabin. Earth is a bright dot now, small enough to cover with a thumbnail. They each make a coffee at the same minute, as if that counted as sharing one.
  Jacques wakes from a dream of rain on a car roof and lies still, trying to keep the sound. The fans hum. The ship ticks as it turns into the sun. By breakfast the rain has gone, and Jacques keeps it to themself.
  01:50. The cabin pressure on Phill's ship has started to fall. Slowly. Phill is the only one awake.
  Around the fleet, Phill, Jacques and Jai kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 11
  Kim went with your call: Save it. Kim saves it for a bad day. Every mission has one, and now Kim has something set aside for it.
  Decision closed: Run it degraded. Votes cast: 3. The fleet goes on water rationing. Nobody complains out loud. Michelle logs the recovery rate every hour, which is its own kind of complaint.
  Behind a stowage panel on Jacques's ship, three ration packs that never made the manifest. Someone at Starbase wrote "you'll thank me" on the bag in Sharpie, next to a little drawing of a Starship with its fingers crossed.
  For whoever at home looks after Jacques, and nobody else: The three packs from behind the panel are the good rations, the ones the galley ran out of first. Nobody else knows they exist.
  Phill spent four hours with an ultrasonic leak detector and a roll of tape. The culprit was a cable feedthrough whose seal shrank in the cold of the shaded side. It's resealed, the pressure holds, and the trend line is taped above the console like a fish someone caught.
  Fleet decision open for 48 hours. Mission Control proposes moving the fleet's clocks two hours forward, to line up with the ground team's shifts. Kim is for it. Jai has seen this study before. [Change the clocks / Keep fleet time]
  For whoever at home looks after Michelle, and nobody else: Michelle finds a hairline crack in a bracket holding a stowage rack, at a corner the model said would carry no stress. A proper fix means opening a ticket with Earth. A zip-tie fix takes five minutes.
  Kim and Jai spend a whole shift arguing on the fleet channel about what plays during the next burn. Kim wants silence, so every noise means something. Jai wants anything with a chorus. They settle on one song, chosen by the ground team, sight unseen. It's worse than either.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Jacques, Phill, Jai and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 12
  Jacques went with your call: Tell the surgeon. The meeting runs on a small pause per question, and everyone learns to ask two at once. Jacques's bunk moves in beside the water tanks, and the reading drops by a third.
  Jai went with your call: Adjust it now. Jai makes the change, watches the wobble die away over six cycles, and writes it down before anyone can ask. The ground signs off in the morning with one word, "Nice", and one attachment: the procedure for next time.
  The galley screen on Phill's ship has started to sulk. On every restart it waits a few seconds for the ground to say hello, gives up, and boots in "offline mode" with a sad little icon. Earth used to answer before the logo finished. Phill files a ticket titled "manners".
  A flare has gone off on the Sun, and its protons will reach the fleet in about forty minutes. Diego packs into the storm shelter between the water tanks with a sleeping bag and a deck of cards, and plays the other six ships over video until the dosimeter settles.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill and Jai.

FLIGHT DAY 13
  Decision closed: Keep fleet time. Votes cast: 3. The fleet keeps its clocks. The ground team adjusts instead, and sends a photo of the night shift's pizza as evidence.
  Fleet decision open for 48 hours. Navigation shows the fleet drifting toward the edge of the arrival corridor. Michelle wants a small correction burn now. Diego wants to wait for the scheduled burn, which costs more but leaves time to check the solution three times. [Burn now / Wait and check]
  Dorian records a message for a school group back home that's been following the fleet. The kids send back forty questions, mostly about the toilet. Dorian answers every one.
  Meanwhile Jai, Phill and Michelle kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 14
  Jacques went with your call: Hand them in. Jacques turns them in, bag and all. The galley has an unplanned surplus for the first time since launch, and the bag goes up on the galley wall.
  Michelle went with your call: Zip-tie it. Michelle lashes the rack in a pattern no design review would pass, and it takes every load the ship can throw at it. The anomaly report calls it "interim". It is not interim.
  After a long debate on the fleet channel, Jacques and Kim get all seven ships to agree on which drawer the zip ties live in. It has taken the whole flight so far, and it may be the most important engineering decision made out here.
  Jai and Michelle compare storm shelters on video and steal each other's best ideas: one more sleeping spot, a hook for the cards, and a sign, now on every ship, that reads "the Sun is not your friend".
  Just before midnight, 214 alarms hit Dorian's console at once. One of them is real.
  Off the main thread, Phill, Jacques, Kim, Jai and Michelle had a full day.

FLIGHT DAY 15
  Decision closed: Wait and check. Votes cast: 5. The solution gets checked three times, then a fourth. The scheduled burn goes off on time and on target, and Diego lets the computer fly it.
  Phill's ship-to-ship receiver, which still scans every channel any Mars craft ever used, logs a carrier too faint to see in any one sweep. Folded over a night at 10.96 seconds, 2.4 percent duty, it stands up out of the noise like a fence post. Phill files it under "retired hardware, probably".
  Fleet decision open for 48 hours. The old-channel carrier is still there, patient as a dripping tap. Phill wants to answer it, if only to be polite: a plain hail on every pass. Jacques points out that every hail turns the big dish off Earth for a few minutes, and the letters from home wait behind a conversation with a machine. [Hail it every pass / Log it, keep going]
  Dorian found it at 01:30, buried on page nine: a coolant pump drawing too much current, minutes from tripping. Dorian swapped to the backup before the primary died. By morning the alarm list had been cut from 214 to a page, and nobody is sure when Dorian slept.
  Around the fleet, Jai and Michelle kept the small things running.

FLIGHT DAY 16
  The galley pump fails for the third time since Starbase. Dorian lays the parts out on a towel in order, rebuilds it, and ends up with one washer it never needed.
  Jacques closes their eyes in the bunk and sees a white spark, then another. Cosmic rays, passing straight through the eye, fast enough to make light; cameras see them too, as single bright pixels. Jacques counts them like sheep and loses count somewhere in the twenties.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Phill and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 17
  Decision closed: Hail it every pass. Votes cast: 2. Phill adds one line to the comms schedule: a plain hail on the old channel, every pass, until someone says stop. The first goes out at 14:02. Then static. The carrier comes and goes as before, and Phill logs the hail times to the second, out of habit.
  The flight surgeon sends Jacques a chart with one line on it, going down. Under it, by hand: "Thank you for telling me." Jacques pins it beside the bunk, between the water tanks.
  The CO₂ scrubber on Kim's ship fails outright. With one person in a cabin that size, the CO₂ only climbs about a twentieth of a percent a day: not an emergency yet, which is the best time to fix one. Kim has the backup running in nine minutes, and it is already complaining.
  Anyone can answer this one. One tap is enough. Kim's ship is breathing on its backup scrubber. It needs a spare cartridge before the backup gives out. [Send a cartridge / Jai brings it over]
  Fleet decision open for 48 hours. A gamma-ray burst goes off somewhere past the galaxy. Timed against the detectors near Earth, the fleet's sighting could pin the source better than ever before, but only if the ships hold attitude through the next window, and that costs propellant. Diego wants the data. Jai wants the propellant. [Hold for the data / Keep the propellant]
  For whoever at home looks after Michelle, and nobody else: An external camera on Michelle's ship has iced over, and its lens heater can't shift it. Clearing it means an unscheduled EVA, alone. Nobody would blame Michelle for waiting for the planned one.
  Phill is aboard Jacques's ship for the transfer window and cuts Jacques's hair with the vacuum clippers. The result is logged as "within tolerance". Jacques agrees and tips in coffee.
  Movie night, with a visitor, on a tablet stuck to the ceiling: Michelle picked a film set on a submarine. Halfway through, the hull ticks as the ship turns out of the sun, right on cue. Michelle pauses it, and the two of them listen until it stops. Nobody presses play again.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill, Michelle, Jacques and Dorian.

FLIGHT DAY 18

  Four of the six pods in the garden tray have gone soft and grey. Dorian lifts them out with tweezers and is quiet for a while. Then Dorian refills the syringe for the other two.
  It's Diego's birthday by the ground calendar, and Michelle is aboard for the transfer window. Michelle produces a candle that can't be lit, a cake that is a ration bar with a slice of dried mango on it, and a card signed by the whole fleet. Diego keeps the candle.

  Meanwhile Michelle, Phill, Kim, Dorian and Diego kept the lights on.

FLIGHT DAY 19

FLIGHT DAY 20

  Diego cleans up the receiver's noise floor for the weekly report and finds a faint ripple in it, at about nine hundredths of a hertz. A ground loop from the coolant pump, most likely: it's on the same bus. Diego notes it in the margin and moves on to the propellant tables.
  Dorian and Michelle try to bake bread in the galley oven while the visit lasts. Without gravity it rises in every direction at once and comes out shaped like a lung. They eat all of it, warm, with the lights down, and agree not to describe it to anyone at home.
  Mission Control misses a scheduled check-in. Then the next one. Phill sends a third hail and starts the clock: twice the light time, plus a minute for someone to put a coffee down.

FLIGHT DAY 40

FLIGHT DAY 41
  04:10. The fleet crosses inside five light-seconds of Mars, the first thing in four years close enough to answer Cargo 2 before its interlock gives up and starts again. Earth's answers always took minutes; it only ever waited eleven seconds. Phill's standing hail goes out on schedule, the same plain words as every pass, and this time it arrives while something is listening. The light stops blinking.
  Entry is tomorrow, straight in from the crossing, into clear air. The landing zone is exactly where the maps said, with one more thing beside it than the maps said. Kim packs a bag for a walk: water, a torch, a roll of tape, a marker. Old habits.
  Diego holds a thumb up to the window, the old habit from the Earth watches. For forty days everything has fitted behind it. Tonight Mars spills out around it on every side, rust and dark, and somewhere on the night side, too small to see, a light is holding steady.
  The last night before entry. Jacques checks the water tanks that shielded the bunk all these weeks, by hand, the way you'd pat a horse. On the fleet channel Jai is doing the same on another ship. Neither of them sleeps much. Both pretend to, so the other one will.
  Elsewhere on the fleet net: Phill, Jacques, Jai and Michelle, busy.

FLIGHT DAY 42
  Jai starts a message to every name on the list from the first call, and one more to the name that isn't there any more. It will land a few minutes after it's sent. Jai leaves the pauses out.
  On the morning of entry Michelle plays the song all the way through on the fleet channel, past the four bars, for the first time since Starbase. Six ships hear the fifth bar at last. It isn't what any of them guessed.
  On the entry map Jai finds the crater the fleet named for a small town at home, right where it should be. Cargo 2 has been standing in its shadow the whole time. Somewhere on Earth, a town newspaper is about to need a second front page.
  On the morning of entry Jacques unpins the empty ration bag from the galley wall and folds it into a pocket, Sharpie side out.
  The rest of the log belongs to Phill, Jacques, Michelle and Dorian.
  OLD FRIEND
  Arrival day. Seven ships come down in clear air, a careful kilometre from a ship that waited four years for someone close enough to answer in time. Its lights hold steady now. On the walk over, Cargo 2 sends its whole manifest, slowly, like something that has been rehearsing. In the hold, the first crate has Sharpie on it, in a hand they know: "You'll thank me." Beside it, a little ship with its fingers crossed.
